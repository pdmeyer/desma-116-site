## Listen and Reflect

Read through these instructions in their entirety before beginning this
exercise.

Spend at least 30 minutes listening to either:

- Annea Lockwood’s _A Sound Map of the Danube_
  <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qwsnWZ4dwz0&t=1394s>
- Steve Reich's _Come Out_ <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0QrZxZzrel8>

**Important: listen on the best pair of headphones you have!**

The point of this exercise is to help you learn to listen with focus. Before
you start listening, set up your space so that you are comfortable and are not
distracted. If possible, try to be alone or in a space where people won’t
bother you. Put away your phone and other devices that might tempt you.
Finally, prepare a pencil and paper so that you can make notes as you listen.

While listening, feel free to make notes about what you’re hearing. It can be
helpful to include timestamps so that you can get back to sections you’d like
to hear again.

After listening, write a 300-500 word reflection on this piece. You can
write about any aspect of the experience that you like - the point is to engage
with your own senses. As you write, point to specific moments as you reflect.
Here are a few questions to inspire you:

- Do these sounds evoke any memories for you? If so, what are they, and why do
  you think they came to mind?
- What sort of space do you imagine these sounds occupy? How big is it and what
  are its qualities?
- Are there any visions or other sensations that you feel while listening?
  These might be visions or other sensations, or even interoceptual or emotional
  sensations
- Perhaps these sounds caused you to think about concepts or abstract ideas. If
  so, what were they, and what do you think it was about these sounds that caused
  you to think this way?
- What words would you use to describe these sounds? Consider that some
  languages have complex vocabularies for describing complex sensations. For
  example, the Jahai people of the Malay Peninsula have a [rich vocabulary for
  smells]
  (<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/s001002771300214x>)
  much like we have words for individual colors. Are you able to invent words to
  describe certain types of sounds that you hear?
- Was there anything in the Eno or Labelle texts or in Lockwood’s lecture that
  sprung to mind while you listened?

To be clear, you shouldn’t answer all of these questions, and you don’t even
need to answer any of them. As mentioned, the point is to engage your senses.
